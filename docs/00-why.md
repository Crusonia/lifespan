# 00 · Why: the output nobody specifies

## The macro picture

Three facts frame the problem.

1. **The US spends the most.** National health expenditure was about $4.9 trillion in 2023, 17.6% of GDP, roughly double the share in 1980. [CMS NHE]
2. **The US gets the least of what matters.** US life expectancy was 78.4 years in 2023 and about 79 in 2024, three to four years below the average of comparable wealthy countries. [NCHS; OECD]
3. **The US has the widest healthspan gap measured.** Across 183 countries, the US had the largest gap between lifespan and healthy lifespan, about 12.4 years, in a 2024 analysis of WHO data. Americans live more of their lives sick than people anywhere else. [Garmany et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024]

Put those together and the system is not short of money or of care. It is short of healthy years, and it spends more every year to get them.

## What the coverage debate measures, and what it doesn't

Medicare for All, a public option, ACA expansion, market-based reform and the status quo are all answers to one question: **who pays, and through what channel?**

That question matters. Coverage changes financial risk, access to care and some health outcomes. But financing is an input. The debate compares inputs, quotes cost estimates, and almost never states the output each plan is expected to deliver, in units that can be checked afterward.

A business that argued about its payment processor and never measured revenue would fail. Health policy has been run that way for decades.

## First principles

Start from what a person actually wants from a health system: **more years, and more of them lived in good health.** Everything else (coverage, visits, prescriptions, procedures, spending) is a means.

From that one premise, four requirements follow.

| Requirement | Why it follows | What it rules out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measure healthy years, not activity | Activity is a cost; healthy years are the product | Counting visits, enrollment or spending as success |
| Count everyone | An average can rise while a large group falls behind | Averages that hide a 10 to 15 year gap between rich and poor |
| Price the outcome | Resources are finite, so the cost of each healthy year matters | Comparing plans by total cost alone |
| Account for time | Some levers work in months, others over decades | Judging long-delay levers inside a 10-year budget window |

These four requirements become the [scoreboard](01-scoreboard.md).

## Why a benchmark, not a plan

A plan asks people to agree. A benchmark asks them to measure.

Plans fail politically because each one bundles a financing choice, which divides people, with an outcome claim, which usually can't be checked. A benchmark separates the two. Anyone can bring any plan. The benchmark scores it on the same axes as every other plan, shows its work, and invites anyone to challenge the score.

The goal is for "how many healthy years does this buy, for whom, at what cost" to become the first question asked of every health proposal, from any side.

## What this document is not

- It is not a financing proposal.
- It is not an argument that care delivery doesn't matter. Care matters, and the benchmark credits it.
- It is not a claim that food or any other single lever solves the problem. The [levers](04-levers.md) are graded on evidence, and some of the evidence is weak.
