# 07 · The strongest counterarguments

A benchmark is only credible if it states the best case against itself. These are the objections that would hurt most if they were ignored.

## 1. "Prevention doesn't save money."

**The objection.** Most preventive interventions add cost rather than saving it. A review of published cost-effectiveness ratios found under 20% of preventive measures were cost-saving, about the same share as for treatments (Cohen, Neumann & Weinstein, NEJM 2008). People who live longer use health care in their added years.

**How the benchmark handles it.** It agrees. That's why [S3](01-scoreboard.md) counts spending in added years and scores cost *per healthy year*, not savings. Prevention is judged the way treatment is: does it buy healthy years at a reasonable price? Some prevention will pass; some won't.

**What remains open.** Whether the large upstream levers (diet, food environment) are cheap enough per healthy year to beat downstream care. That's an empirical question.

## 2. "The food evidence is mostly observational."

**The objection.** Diet–disease links come largely from cohort studies, which are vulnerable to confounding. People who eat well also exercise, earn more and smoke less. Nutrition science has reversed itself before.

**How the benchmark handles it.** It grades food levers C for disease outcomes, lower than coverage or the DPP, and [S5](01-scoreboard.md) caps a plan's confidence at its weakest important link. The benchmark does not assume food works. It defines what evidence would show whether it does.

## 3. "Coverage saves lives. Calling it the 'smallest lever' is an excuse to do nothing."

**The objection.** Good studies show coverage expansions reduce mortality. Tens of millions are uninsured or underinsured. Telling them to eat better is not a health plan.

**How the benchmark handles it.** It credits coverage with every healthy year the evidence supports, and coverage gaps where cost per healthy year is low are part of the [frontier portfolio](05-plans-vs-the-frontier.md). The claim is not that coverage doesn't matter. It is that coverage alone leaves the largest slice of the gap untouched. Both are true.

## 4. "Healthy life expectancy is too soft to measure."

**The objection.** HALE depends on self-reported health or modeled disability weights. WHO and IHME disagree by one to two years. Changing the definition changes the answer.

**How the benchmark handles it.** It reports both series and their disagreement, always shows plain life expectancy next to HALE, and uses a narrower, biomarker-based proxy (metabolic health) where it can. The definition is versioned so it can't be shifted to fit a conclusion.

## 5. "The frontier is unfair. The US isn't Japan."

**The objection.** Comparisons to other countries ignore differences in population, geography, culture and diversity.

**How the benchmark handles it.** That's why the domestic frontier (F2) exists. Americans in the healthiest income groups and counties already live as long as, or longer than, the best countries. The gap is not American biology.

## 6. "A lot of the gap is overdoses, guns and cars. That isn't a health-system problem."

**The objection.** If external causes drive much of the US life expectancy gap, then neither financing nor food plans are the main story.

**How the benchmark handles it.** It agrees this is a large part of the life expectancy gap, reports it as its own slice (G2), and scores every plan on it, including the upstream portfolio, which barely touches it. The benchmark does not claim to be about food. It is about healthy years.

## 7. "Whoever built this has an interest in the answer."

**The objection.** The benchmark comes out of the Food is Health newsletter and is maintained by people who invest in food and health companies. A scoreboard built by an interested party will favor its own levers.

**How the benchmark handles it.** By disclosure and by construction. Conflicts are disclosed in [GOVERNANCE](../GOVERNANCE.md). The scoring rules apply identically to every plan, the evidence grades are published, and the preliminary scoring rates food levers' confidence *below* coverage. Anyone can rerun the model, challenge a grade or submit a rival scoring. If the rules favor one side, that should be visible and fixable in public.

## 8. "Systems models can be made to say anything."

**The objection.** System dynamics models have many parameters and feedback loops. With enough freedom, they reproduce whatever their builders believe.

**How the benchmark handles it.** Loops stay labeled as premises until they have evidence and equations. Parameters are registered with sources and uncertainty. Tornado diagrams show which inputs drive each result. Synthetic results are labeled validation-only. Historical backtests report their errors rather than tuning them away.
